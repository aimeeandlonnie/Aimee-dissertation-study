Detailed Information

The following is more detailed information about this study in addition to the information listed above.

What is the purpose of this research?

This research asks four specific questions in learning: (1) How do evidence and prior beliefs interact in children's causal learning? (2) What are the early developmental origins of construing and understanding events? (3) What are learning algorithms that include appropriate cognitive developmental constraints? (4) What is the role of social information in learning? This research examines the cognitive components and learning systems underlying the development and structure of both the response and detection of novel stimuli and the formation and revision of causal belief in humans across the lifespan and how these experiences may change reasoning.

The Houses of Velora

Velora is an invented ancient civilization. This lesson describes how Velorans were bound to civic Houses, how they understood those bonds, and how their Stone-keepers settled disagreements about them.

Houses and Marks

Velorans organized public life around Houses, civic groups whose members shared duties. Tor kept the lanterns: its members carried them at the Festival of First Rain and repaired the lantern hall. Seli kept the table: its members prepared the festival meal and maintained the ceremonial kitchen. A person's tie to a House was recorded as a House Mark, carved beside their name on the great record stone in the city square and tended by officials called Stone-keepers. A Mark recorded an obligation. It was not a family name or a place of residence, and working in a House's building or helping with its tasks did not by itself grant its Mark. Every Veloran received a first Mark in childhood; apprenticeship, marriage, or public service could later bind them to another House.

Alignment and the Turning of the Stone

A Veloran could hold several Marks at once, but not every obligation was equally strong. Alignment describes the strength of a person's obligation to a House. When two Houses required incompatible actions at the same time, the House with the stronger alignment came first. Stone-keepers called this the law of alignment, and under it, receiving a Mark first did not by itself give that Mark greater priority.

A person changed their alignment through a ceremony called the Turning of the Stone. The Turning strengthened alignment toward a new House and weakened alignment toward an earlier one. It changed which obligation came first without canceling the earlier Mark. A weaker obligation stayed active whenever fulfilling it did not interfere with a stronger one: someone with stronger Seli alignment would prepare the meal if both Houses needed help at once, but on a day when Seli had nothing for them, they would still owe help to Tor. Lower priority meant a weaker obligation, not no obligation.

The Turning of the Stone also changed the record stone itself. During the ceremony, a Stone-keeper chiseled away the person's earlier Mark and carved the new Mark in its place. Once the earlier Mark was gone from the stone, every obligation it had recorded ended with it. After the Turning, only the new Mark was active, and the person owed nothing to their former House, even on days when their new House had no task for them.

The cradle rule

Velorans did not first learn about Marks from the Stone-keepers. Rather, each formed a reckoning long before they interacted with the Stone-keepers: a working explanation of which Houses a person must serve, and whose duty comes first when two Houses need them at once. Children's reckonings grew from what they saw around them, and because most children watched adults serve the House of their childhood, many grew up holding the cradle rule: a person must always put first the House whose Mark they received first.

Learning the law of alignment did not make the cradle rule disappear. Adults often kept both, reaching for the cradle rule in some situations and for the law of alignment in others, without ever setting the two side by side. Some could recite the law of alignment perfectly yet fall back on the cradle rule when they met an unfamiliar case.

Adding and recutting: Changing a reckoning

There are two ways a reckoning could change: Adding meant taking in new information that fit a reckoning already held, such as learning one more duty that Tor members performed. Recutting meant changing the rule a reckoning used to decide which Houses a person must serve or whose duty comes first. A Veloran who stopped deciding priority by which Mark came first, and began deciding it by alignment, had recut their reckoning. Stone-keepers trusted that a recut had taken hold only when the person applied the new rule to someone they had never considered before.

Nera and the two requests

Nera received a Tor Mark as a child. Years later, she apprenticed with Seli and completed the Turning of the Stone, and her alignment toward Seli became stronger than her alignment toward Tor. At the next Festival of First Rain, both Houses needed her at the same hour, and Nera prepared the meal with Seli.

A week later, a storm damaged Tor's lantern hall, and Tor asked Nera to help with repairs. Seli had no work for her that day, and the repairs would not interfere with any Seli duty. Whether Nera had to help Tor that day depended on what the Turning had done to her Tor Mark, not on which task she had chosen at the festival.

The Weighing

When Velorans disagreed about which duty someone had to perform, Stone-keepers held a Weighing. They imagined one particular person at one particular moment, traced what each claim required of that person, and compared the results. Person, time and circumstances had to stay fixed, because weighing one rule at the festival and another on a different day could hide a disagreement. Weighing the cradle rule against the law of alignment at the festival, for example, shows at once that the first sends Nera to Tor's lanterns and the second sends her to Seli's kitchen.

A Weighing added no new custom, and on its own it did not settle which claim was right. Its value lay in making visible what claims already held would require. Stone-keepers called the habit of checking whether all of one's claims could hold together truing. Two claims could each sound familiar yet demand incompatible answers in the same imagined case, and finding such a disagreement was a reason to reexamine the claims or ask for clarification.

About the lesson

About the Lesson You Just Read

Thank you for participating. This lesson builds on a long-standing interest of cognitive science in how learners revise their beliefs when their existing explanations fall short. This lesson was intentionally written to be somewhat confusing (including providing potentially contradicting information about whether competing beliefs are overwritten during learning or maintained but believed to a lesser extent), in order to encourage participants to ask clarifying questions. (You may have been in a baseline condition where there was no mechanism to ask questions.)

This lesson introduced key ideas in cognitive development (though note that experts can disagree about these definitions). Here, we use belief revision to capture any kind of learning that involves updating beliefs, and conceptual change captures cases when those beliefs are a causal model of the world or an intuitive theory that gets radically revised. Consistency monitoring is when a learner holds two beliefs that lead to contradictory predictions about the same situation and notices that both beliefs cannot be true. Enrichment is a type of belief revision when a learner adds new information that fits with what they already understand, without needing to overwrite their original explanation. Conceptual replacement is the idea that a new belief completely writes over an old one (that the original revised belief is no longer accessible). Thought experiments support learning by asking the student to imagine a situation and trace what each explanation would predict, which makes hidden contradictions easier to notice.
